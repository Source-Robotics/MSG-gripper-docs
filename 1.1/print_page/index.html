    Before you start

    Quick start¶

    From an assembled MSG gripper to its first gentle open and close, using Motor GUI on a PC. For a gripper already connected to a robot, use On PAROL6 and PAR6.

    Before connecting power

    Keep fingers and loose objects out of the stroke. Calibration opens and closes the jaws fully. A wrong cable can put 24 V on the CAN pins and destroy the driver: check the numbered pinout.

    1. Choose your starting point¶

    Your gripper Start here
    Bought assembled Its stepper is already calibrated and the driver configured. Continue below; the jaws still need calibration after each power-up.
    Self-built, or a replacement driver or stepper Finish Driver setup and first run first, then return here. Do not apply a factory preset to a bought gripper just to try this page.
    No hardware yet Open Motor GUI, choose Mock CAN (demo) and Connect, then switch to Gripper. The demo is an SSG-48; the control panel is shared with MSG.

    2. Gather and mount¶

    You need a mounted gripper with jaws/fingers fitted, a 24 V supply, a correctly wired M8 cable, a CANvas USB-to-CAN adapter, and Chrome or Edge. The What you need page lists the parts; Mounting and Jaws cover the mechanics.

    Check: the body cannot shift, the jaws travel freely, and the cable cannot enter their path.

    3. Wire with power off¶

    1. Check the cable against Connector and pinout: gripper pin 3 is +24 V, pin 4 GND, pin 2 CANH, pin 1 CANL. Check continuity before the first power-up.
    2. Connect the supply and the CANvas adapter as shown below.
    3. Set the gripper's CAN termination ON for this two-node bench bus; the CANvas adapter provides the other end. Leave the gripper at its shipped node ID, 0, unless you already assigned another.
    flowchart LR
    PC[PC USB] --> A[CANvas adapter]
    A -- CANH --> P2[Gripper pin 2: CANH]
    A -- CANL --> P1[Gripper pin 1: CANL]
    S[24 V supply] -- positive --> P3[Gripper pin 3: +24 V]
    S -- return --> P4[Gripper pin 4: GND]

    Check, unpowered: the supply polarity and cable pinout match, and CANH-to-CANL measures about 60 Ω with the bus connected. Otherwise check The cable and CAN termination before powering it.

    4. Connect Motor GUI¶

    Power the gripper, plug the CANvas adapter into USB, and open motorgui.com. Choose CAN (slcan) at 1 Mbit/s, press Connect, and choose the adapter's serial port. Select node 0 (or your assigned ID) and switch from Motor to Gripper.

    Check: position and current feedback arrive. Not calibrated after power-up is normal. If feedback is missing, check the power, port, node ID and termination: Troubleshooting. If a fault disables the controls, resolve it using Errors and recovery. The illustrated Motor GUI guide shows the panel.

    5. Calibrate and activate¶

    With the stroke empty and the gripper deactivated, press Calibrate, read the prompt, and press Start calibration. Let the jaws find both end stops. Follow Calibrate, activate, move for the full procedure, then Activate and check for faults.

    Check: the panel says Calibrated, Activated, and No errors before you command a move. A calibration that times out after 24 seconds has not found its end stops; do not continue as if it passed. Use Calibration troubleshooting.

    6. Make the first move¶

    Set Speed 30 and Current 300 mA, applying each with its Set button. Press Open, then Close. After that works, put a light test object between the jaws and repeat, keeping fingers clear.

    Check: the position follows the command; on an object the jaws stop before fully closed and the panel reports an object. If it does not move, slips or reports an error, use Troubleshooting. The current limit sets an approximate force limit; Force and speed explains the relationship and the motor's limits.

    7. Finish and continue¶

    Remove the test object before deactivating or switching off: the grip may release. Disconnect Motor GUI and switch off the supply before changing cables.

    1. Overview

    Overview¶

    What the MSG gripper is and how it works, the variants it comes in, and what you need around it to get it gripping.

    In this chapter¶

    • The gripper at a glance
    • Variants
    • What you need

    The gripper at a glance¶

    The MSG is a two-jaw parallel gripper driven by a NEMA 17 stepper under field-oriented control. The STEPFOC driver on the back of the stepper regulates the motor's current, so a move sets not only where the jaws go and how fast, but how hard they press: the current limit is the grip force. The jaws stop on an object, hold it with that force, and the driver reports that an object was found. We designed the MSG specifically for teleoperation, VLA (vision-language-action) training and physical intelligence. A camera on top of the body looks down between the jaws, from a place chosen for a clear view of both jaws and the object between them.

    The parts¶

    Part What it does
    NEMA 17 stepper motor, 21.5, 40 or 60 mm long the actuator; its length sets the grip force: about 31, 50 or 76 N at its maximum current
    STEPFOC driver on the back of the stepper field-oriented control of the stepper, the gripper state machine, the CAN and UART interfaces
    Pinion on the motor shaft, two racks on a linear rail the pinion turns, the two racks move in opposite directions: the jaws open and close symmetrically
    MGN7 linear rail, 100, 150 or 200 mm carries the jaws; its length sets the stroke
    Jaws printed, in several shapes, with a soft TPU pad or solid; yours to adapt
    Thermistor on the motor housing temperature protection: the gripper stops at 75 °C
    M8 4-pin connector 24 V and the CAN bus, one cable to the robot
    Camera mount an OV9732 USB camera module looking between the jaws
    Coupler plate mounts the gripper on an 8 mm shaft, or on the PAROL6 wrist with its own cover

    The driver's status LED and its UART port are inside the housing; the CAN termination switch is reachable through the stepper cover (CAN termination).

    What the firmware does¶

    After power-up the gripper calibrates by finding both end stops. Then it takes move commands: a position from 0 (open) to 255 (closed), a speed from 0 to 255 and a current limit in mA. When the jaws stop short of the target with the current at its limit, an object is in the way and is held; when they reach the target, the position is held. Every answer carries the state: moving, object found while closing or opening, or at the position. The gripper stops itself on an over-temperature, an E-stop bit from the host, a CAN watchdog that runs out, and every fault the driver detects. How the gripper works and Errors and recovery have the details.

    How it is controlled¶

    • CAN, 1 Mbit/s, on the same cable as the power: one message moves the gripper and is answered with its state. From a PC with the CANvas adapter, from a ROS 2 node, from PAROL6 or PAR6, or from your own controller. See Over CAN.
    • Python, the Spectral-BLDC library on top of CAN. See With Python.
    • Motor GUI in the browser: connect, calibrate, open and close, watch the plots. See Motor GUI.
    • UART, the driver's serial port inside the housing: for configuration, and for a self-built gripper. See Over UART.

    Variants¶

    The gripper is modular in two ways: the length of the linear rail sets the stroke, the length of the stepper sets the force. Any rail goes with any stepper, and the body comes in black or grey; the body, the jaw carriers and the stepper cover are printed for the chosen combination.

    Rail length and stroke¶

    Rail Stroke Printed parts
    100 mm 55 mm MSG_Gripper_body_100mm, gripper_jaw_100mm_L/R
    150 mm 105 mm MSG_Gripper_body_150mm, gripper_jaw_150mm_L/R
    200 mm 155 mm MSG_Gripper_body_200mm, gripper_jaw_200mm_L/R

    The stroke is the distance between the jaw carriers from fully open to fully closed; the opening between the jaw faces depends on the jaws you fit (Jaws). A longer rail makes the gripper wider and a little heavier and does not change the force.

    Stepper length and force¶

    Stepper Motor Maximum current Assumed torque constant Ideal combined force at the maximum current Printed parts
    21.5 mm 17HS08-1004S 1.5 A 0.17 Nm/A about 31 N Stepper_cover_21_5mm, Stepper_spacer_21_5
    40 mm 17HS16-2004S1 2.0 A 0.21 Nm/A about 50 N Stepper_cover_40mm
    60 mm 17HS24-2104S 2.1 A 0.30 Nm/A about 76 N Stepper_cover_60mm

    A longer stepper gives more torque and so more grip force, at the price of weight and length behind the gripper. The force is proportional to the current limit you command, with the slope of the stepper's torque constant; the curves and the formula are in Force and speed. The current limit must stay within the stepper's maximum.

    Grasps¶

    The gripper grips from the outside (the jaws close on the object) and from the inside (the jaws open inside a hollow object). Both are the same move command with a different target; object detection reports while closing for the first and while opening for the second.

    The camera¶

    The camera mount on top of the body carries an OV9732 USB camera module (100° version in the BOM), looking down between the jaws. Its place and angle were chosen with care: the camera sees both jaws and the object between them, the view that teleoperation and VLA training need. It is a plain USB camera with its own cable, independent of the gripper's electronics; the mount and its lid are printed parts (camera extension, camera_lid), and a gripper without a camera simply leaves them off.

    What you need¶

    The gripper¶

    The MSG gripper is sold assembled, calibrated and configured in the Source Robotics shop, in the rail and stepper combination you choose, in black or grey. It ships with the current STEPFOC firmware and the bootloader installed, its driver in gripper mode and its stepper calibrated; the gripper calibration (finding the end stops) runs after every power-up and is part of normal use.

    Or build it. The MSG is open source, and that is important to us: the CAD and print files, the bill of materials, the assembly video, the firmware and the software are all published. The build guide is the order to do it in.

    What is in the box¶

    The gripper, assembled, calibrated and configured, in the rail, stepper and colour you ordered, with the camera module when ordered. Not in the box: the jaws, which you print from the repository or buy in the shop; the cable, sold separately; a CAN adapter for a PC.

    A cable and a 24 V supply¶

    The gripper is powered and controlled through one M8 4-pin cable: 24 V, GND, CANH, CANL.

    Where the gripper goes Cable
    PAROL6 the PAROL6 gripper cable from the shop
    PAR6 the PAR6 gripper cable (the shop; coming soon), wired differently from the PAROL6 one
    a bench, or your own robot a cable you make from an M8 4-pin connector and four wires, see The cable

    The two robot cables differ

    PAROL6 and PAR6 put the four signals on different pins. A cable for the wrong robot, or a self-made cable with a wrong pin, puts 24 V where the CAN bus belongs and destroys the gripper's driver. Check the pinout before you plug in a cable for the first time.

    On the bench, a 24 V supply with a current limit (2 A is plenty for one gripper) is the safest source.

    A CAN interface¶

    The gripper talks CAN. On a robot, the robot's control board provides the bus. On a PC, the CANvas USB-to-CAN adapter does; it shows up as a serial port and works with Motor GUI, the Python library and the ROS 2 driver. Any adapter that python-can supports works with the library.

    Software¶

    • Motor GUI, motorgui.com, in the browser over the CANvas adapter: the recommended tool to test and set up a gripper, with a gripper panel for the MSG and the SSG-48; see Motor GUI. The earlier SSG-gripper-GUI, a Python program, is legacy.
    • Python: pip install Spectral-BLDC, the library the ROS 2 driver is built on; see With Python.
    • ROS 2: the msg_gripper_ros2 package; see With ROS 2.
    • PAROL6 and PAR6: the robots' own software drives the gripper; see On PAROL6 and PAR6.
    • For a self-built gripper: a 3.3 V USB-to-serial adapter (serial adapter) for the driver's UART port, to configure the driver with Motor GUI or a terminal; see Driver configuration. For a self-build we recommend the STEPFOC starter kit: it has this adapter and the rest of what the driver's setup needs (Kits and the shop).

    Optional¶

    • Jaws for your objects: the printed jaws in the repository (solid, hollow, or with a TPU pad), sponge tape for the gripping faces, or your own design; see Jaws.
    • A camera: an OV9732 USB module for the camera mount; see Variants.

    Safety¶

    The MSG gripper is a force-controlled gripper for collaborative and research robots, not a certified safety component. Whoever puts it on a machine is responsible for the risk assessment of the gripper, the robot and everything around them. Read this chapter before you power the gripper; the rules here are the ones that injure a hand or destroy the gripper when they are ignored.

    Mechanical¶

    The jaws

    The jaws are a pinch point. With the current limit at the stepper's rating they close with tens of newtons, enough to crush a finger, and at full speed they travel the whole stroke in well under a second. Never put a finger between the jaws of a powered gripper. Keep hands away during calibration, which moves the jaws over the whole stroke without any command from you.

    Start low

    Test every new setup with a low current and speed: current 300 to 500 mA, speed 30. Raise them step by step once the application behaves, and never above the stepper's rated current (Force and speed).

    Objects

    A gripped object that slips, or that the jaws crush, can fly or fall. The grip force is a current limit, not a measurement: a hard object takes the full force, a soft one deforms until the force is reached.

    Electrical¶

    The cable

    The gripper's M8 connector carries 24 V next to the CAN bus. A cable with the wrong pinout, including the cable of the other Source Robotics robot, puts 24 V on the CAN pins and destroys the driver. Use the cable made for your robot or check every pin of your own; see The cable.

    Supply

    24 V DC. The driver inside tolerates 12 to 29 V, but the gripper's force and speed figures assume 24 V.

    Do not unplug a powered gripper

    Connect and disconnect the cable with the supply off. Hot-plugging the connector can damage the driver.

    Temperature¶

    A thermistor on the motor housing measures the motor's temperature. At 75 °C the driver raises a temperature error and the gripper goes idle; it clears when the motor has cooled and the error is cleared. A gripper holding an object at a high current for long heats up: for a long hold, use the lowest current that holds the object. A self-built gripper without the thermistor on its motor has no protection and can burn out its motor; see Driver configuration.

    Stopping the gripper¶

    • The E-stop bit of the move command (Send_gripper_data_pack) stops the gripper and raises the gripper E-stop error, which stays until it is cleared.
    • Send_ESTOP to the driver, or #Idle over UART, stops it as well.
    • The CAN watchdog (Send_Watchdog) stops the gripper by itself when the host stops talking, which is the right protection for a program that may crash mid-grip.
    • Cutting the supply releases the grip: a stepper without current holds nothing beyond its own friction, so an object can drop.

    None of these is a certified safety function. A machine that can hurt someone needs an emergency stop that cuts the power, independent of the gripper and the robot software.

    Care¶

    The gripper is not waterproof or dustproof: the rail and the racks are open to dust, chips and liquids. Keep it out of wet or dusty places. What to check, and when to grease the mechanism, is in Maintenance.

    Liability¶

    The MSG gripper is sold as a component for people who build their own machines, and its designs are published for people who build the gripper itself. The hardware designs, the firmware and this documentation are under development and may contain errors. Whoever builds or operates a machine with the gripper is responsible for its safe design, assembly and operation. The full disclaimer is in the product repository.

    Force and speed¶

    A move command carries a current limit in mA and a speed from 0 to 255. This page estimates the resulting force and speed. Force and payload values are ideal model estimates, not measured ratings.

    Grip force¶

    The driver limits the motor's current to the value you command, so the torque the motor produces is limited to \(T = K_t \, I_q\), with \(K_t\) the motor's torque constant in Nm/A (#Kt prints what the driver uses) and \(I_q\) the current limit in amperes (the command is in mA). The pinion shares that torque between two racks. Here \(F\) is the combined normal force of both jaws, not the force at one jaw:

    \[ F = \frac{K_t \, I_q}{r}, \qquad r = 8.3\ \text{mm} \]

    \(r\) is the pitch radius of the pinion (32 DP, 21 teeth). The force is proportional to the current, with a slope set by the stepper:

    Stepper Motor Assumed \(K_t\) Ideal combined force per ampere Maximum current Ideal combined force at the maximum current
    21.5 mm 17HS08-1004S 0.17 Nm/A 20 N/A 1.5 A about 31 N
    40 mm 17HS16-2004S1 0.21 Nm/A 25 N/A 2.0 A about 50 N
    60 mm 17HS24-2104S 0.30 Nm/A 36 N/A 2.1 A about 76 N

    The curves use the listed torque constants; they are not force-gauge measurements. STEPFOC estimates #Kt from a no-load speed/voltage test, rather than measuring shaft torque. The constant must match the driver's \(q\)-axis current convention; a motor datasheet's current rating or supply current cannot be substituted directly. A self-built gripper with another stepper needs its own force calibration. Below about 150 mA the motor does not move reliably, so the usable range starts at a few newtons. Motor GUI commands up to 1500 mA; the full force of the 40 mm and 60 mm steppers needs a command from Python or over CAN.

    The current limit caps motor torque; it does not guarantee that the commanded current or the estimated contact force is reached. Mechanism friction, finger flex and the contact position affect the actual force.

    Stay within the stepper's maximum

    The firmware does not clamp the gripper's current. A current above the stepper's maximum overheats the motor; the thermistor stops the gripper at 75 °C, but a motor driven far over its rating heats faster than the thermistor follows. The maximums are in the table above.

    For a symmetric grip, each jaw applies \(F/2\). The torque balance is \(T = F_\mathrm{left} r + F_\mathrm{right} r = F r\); counting \(F\) once for each jaw would count the motor's torque twice. These are theoretical forces before losses in the mechanism. A compression gauge between the jaws reads the force at one contact; double that reading before comparing it with the combined force here. Record the current, fingers and contact position when measuring; the same setup is needed for a useful comparison.

    Payload¶

    The grip force is not the weight the gripper can lift. An object held by friction between the two jaws is held by \(\mu(F_\mathrm{left} + F_\mathrm{right}) = \mu F\), with \(\mu\) the friction coefficient between the jaw faces and the object. Dividing the available friction force by a chosen safety factor \(S\) gives the static estimate:

    \[ W = \frac{\mu\,F}{S}, \qquad m = \frac{W}{g} \]

    Using an assumed \(\mu\) = 0.3 and \(S\) = 2, the model gives about 11 N of supported weight (1.2 kg) at 76 N of combined grip force, or 4.7 N (0.47 kg) at 31 N. These are static estimates. Upward acceleration of 1 g doubles the load; acceleration and emergency-stop loads need a separate check. Friction can be lower than 0.3, so measure it with your jaws on your object. A form-fit grip needs its own load assessment.

    Speed¶

    The speed byte of a move is mapped by the firmware to the motor's speed in encoder ticks per second, linearly from 500 at speed 0 to 60000 at speed 255:

    \[ \text{ticks/s} = 500 + \frac{60000 - 500}{255}\, C \]

    The motor's encoder has 16384 ticks per revolution, so the angular speed is \(\omega = \text{ticks/s} / 16384 \times 2\pi\) and each jaw moves at

    \[ v = \omega \, r \]
    Speed byte Motor speed Each jaw moves at The jaws close at
    0 500 ticks/s, 0.03 rev/s 1.6 mm/s 3 mm/s
    30 7500 ticks/s, 0.46 rev/s 24 mm/s 48 mm/s
    128 30400 ticks/s, 1.9 rev/s 97 mm/s 190 mm/s
    255 60000 ticks/s, 3.7 rev/s 190 mm/s 380 mm/s

    Both jaws move, so the gap closes at twice the speed of one jaw. At speed 255 a 105 mm stroke takes about half a second. The gripper calibration runs at speed 40 and 500 mA.

    Position¶

    The position byte is linear over the stroke: 0 is the fully open end stop found by the gripper calibration, 255 the fully closed one. One step of the byte is the stroke divided by 255: 0.22 mm on the 100 mm rail, 0.41 mm on the 150 mm rail, 0.61 mm on the 200 mm rail. The driver considers the position reached within 5 encoder ticks, about 0.03 mm.

    A strip of 1 mm sponge tape (30 mm wide, in the BOM) on the gripping face of any jaw improves the hold on hard and smooth objects a great deal. Cut it to length, peel the backing and press it on.

    Keep the carrier's hole pattern from one of the designs and shape the rest for your objects. Long jaws multiply the lever arm on the carriers: keep them short and stiff, and remember that the grip force is set at the jaw faces, not at the rail.

    On a PAR6, custom jaws must keep the size of the original jaws: no longer, no wider and no thicker. The PAR6 software works with the gripper's original dimensions (its collision model of the tool and the tool centre point), so a larger jaw reaches where the robot does not expect it.

    Calibrate after changing the jaws¶

    The gripper calibration finds the end stops with the jaws fitted; jaws that meet earlier or later than the old ones change where 255 is. Calibrate again after a change, which happens at the next power-up anyway.

    Calibrate, activate, move¶

    Every session with the gripper starts the same way: calibrate, activate, clear what errors there are. Then it moves. This page does it once with Motor GUI, with Python and over UART; How the gripper works explains what happens inside.

    Danger

    The jaws move over the whole stroke during calibration, and close with the set force afterwards. Nothing in reach of them, fingers included.

    1. Power up¶

    At power-up the gripper reports Not calibrated and does not move. Its stepper must be calibrated too, which a bought gripper is from the factory; a self-built gripper gets that in Driver configuration.

    2. Calibrate¶

    The gripper calibration finds the two end stops: the jaws open fully, pause, then close fully, at low force (500 mA) and low speed. Each end is recognised when the jaws have stood still against it for half a second. It takes about ten seconds; the result is not kept over a power cycle, so this runs at every power-up.

    Calibrate, and confirm. When the jaws stop, the panel shows Calibrated.

    gripper.Send_gripper_calibrate()
time.sleep(10)

    #Gripcal

    If the jaws do not move at all, or move to one end and stop: the gripper is in an error state (clear it first, see step 4), or the stepper is not calibrated. If the routine runs for 24 seconds and gives up, an end stop was not found: something blocks the jaws short of the end, or the mechanism is too stiff for 500 mA.

    3. Activate¶

    A calibrated gripper carries out a move only while it is activated.

    Activate. The panel shows Activated.

    The activate bit is part of every move command, its fourth argument; send it as 1. To activate without moving, send a command with the action bit, the fifth argument, at 0:

    gripper.Send_gripper_data_pack(0, 30, 300, 1, 0, 0, 0)   # position, speed, current, activate = 1, action = 0, E-stop, release direction

    #Grippos activates by itself; #Gripact 1 activates without moving.

    4. Clear errors¶

    The driver powers up in an error state until its motor is calibrated and it has run through its checks; an E-stop, an over-temperature or a supply fault leave an error too. A gripper with an error does not move.

    Clear errors, when the panel shows an error.

    gripper.Send_Clear_Error()

    #Clear

    An error whose cause is still present comes straight back; Errors and recovery says what each needs.

    5. Move¶

    A move is three numbers: the position (0 open, 255 closed), the speed (0 to 255: linear from 1.6 to 190 mm/s at each jaw) and the current limit in mA, which is the grip force (Force and speed). Start with speed 30, about 24 mm/s at each jaw, and 300 mA.

    Set Speed 30 and Current 300 with their Set buttons, then Close and Open, or set a Position: the jaws follow, the plots show the position and the current, and the state says whether the jaws reached the position or stopped on something.

    gripper.Send_gripper_data_pack(200, 30, 300, 1, 1, 0, 0)   # position, speed, current, activate, action, E-stop, release direction
time.sleep(2)
gripper.Send_gripper_data_pack(20, 30, 300, 1, 1, 0, 0)

    #Gripvel 30
#Gripcur 300
#Grippos 200
#Grippos 20

    Put an object between the jaws and close on it: the jaws stop on it and hold it with 300 mA, and the state reads object detected while closing. Raise the current for a firmer grip, within the stepper's rating, and the speed once the motion is what you want.

    What the gripper reports¶

    Every move command is answered with the gripper's state, which Motor GUI shows in its panel and the library puts into attributes: the position, the current, whether it is calibrated and activated, whether a move is in progress, the object-detection result, and the three error bits. How the gripper works goes through them.

    Operation¶

    How the gripper works¶

    The gripper is a state machine in the driver's firmware, fed by one command and answering with one status. This page is what it does with them.

    The states¶

    stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> NotCalibrated: power-up
    NotCalibrated --> Calibrating: Send_gripper_calibrate / #Gripcal
    Calibrating --> Calibrated: both end stops found
    Calibrating --> NotCalibrated: 24 s without an end stop
    Calibrated --> Moving: command with activate = 1, action = 1
    Moving --> Holding: object found, or position reached
    Holding --> Moving: a different command
    Moving --> Idle: error, E-stop, action = 0
    Holding --> Idle: error, E-stop, action = 0
    Idle --> Moving: command, errors cleared

    Not calibrated

    • After power-up the gripper does not know where its jaws are.
    • It answers status requests and refuses moves.
    • The gripper calibration is not kept over a power cycle.

    Calibrating

    • The jaws open at speed 40 and 500 mA until they have stood still against the end stop for half a second.
    • They pause briefly, then close the same way.
    • The two end positions become 0 (open) and 255 (closed).
    • If an end stop is not found within 24 seconds, the routine gives up and the gripper stays uncalibrated.
    • Nothing else is accepted while it runs; the action bit of the status reads 0.

    Moving

    • A command with the activate and action bits set starts a move towards the position, at the speed.
    • The current is limited to the commanded value.
    • The driver runs its velocity loop towards the target and watches two things: the speed of the jaws and the motor current.

    Holding: the move ends in one of two ways.

    • Position reached (within 5 encoder ticks): the driver switches to position control and holds the jaws there. Object detection reads 3.
    • Object in the way: the jaws stop before the target with the current at its limit. The driver keeps pushing with the limited current, which is the grip. Object detection reads 1 if the jaws were closing, 2 if they were opening.
    • Either way, nothing moves until a different command arrives.

    Idle

    • An error, the E-stop bit, or a command with the action bit at 0 takes the gripper to idle.
    • The motor is no longer driven and holds nothing beyond the mechanism's friction, so an object can slip.
    • Clearing the error, or the next command with the action bit set, moves it again.

    The command¶

    Send_gripper_data_pack carries, in 5 bytes:

    Field Range Meaning
    Position 0 to 255 0 fully open, 255 fully closed, linear over the stroke
    Speed 0 to 255 1.6 to 190 mm/s at each jaw, linear (500 to 60000 encoder ticks per second); see Force and speed
    Current mA the current limit of the move, so the grip force; not clamped by the firmware
    Activate bit 1 activates the gripper; 0 deactivates it, and it stops
    Action bit 1 carries out the move; 0 stores the command and leaves the gripper idle
    E-stop bit 1 stops the gripper and raises the E-stop error
    Release direction bit read by the firmware but not used

    The same command sent twice does nothing new: the driver compares every command with the previous one, and only a changed command resets the "at position" state and starts a move. So a host may repeat the command at any rate, which the CAN watchdog needs; to move the jaws from a held position to the same position again, change the speed or the current by one.

    An empty Send_gripper_data_pack, 0 bytes, changes nothing and is answered like a full one: it is the status poll, and it costs the bus almost nothing. A host usually sends one full command per move and polls with empty ones.

    Over UART, #Grippos is the same command: it sets the position, activates the gripper, sets the action bit and starts the move, with the speed and current last set by #Gripvel and #Gripcur (50 and 400 mA after power-up).

    The answer¶

    Every Send_gripper_data_pack is answered with Respond_Gripper_data_pack, 4 bytes:

    Field Meaning
    Position where the jaws are, 0 to 255
    Current the motor current, mA; positive while opening, negative while closing
    Activated the activate flag
    Action status 1 while a move is in progress, 0 when idle, holding, or calibrating
    Object detection 0 moving, 1 object found while closing, 2 object found while opening, 3 at the position
    Temperature error the motor's thermistor is above the limit
    Timeout error the driver's CAN watchdog ran out
    E-stop error the E-stop bit was received
    Calibrated the gripper calibration is done

    The bits are listed in Errors and status bits, with the byte layout in the CAN reference.

    What a host sends and what comes back, from power-up to a held object:

    Object detection, exactly¶

    While a move is in progress and the jaws are not yet at the target, the driver reads moving (0) as long as the jaws move faster than 500 encoder ticks per second, about 1.6 mm/s. When they are slower than that and the motor current is above 80 % of the commanded limit, something stops them: object found, 1 or 2 by the sign of the current. Below 80 % with the jaws slow, it still reads moving: the jaws are accelerating or the object is soft and still being squeezed. Once the target is reached, 3, and it stays 3 until a different command arrives.

    The state is therefore a snapshot of each answer. A host that wants to know "did the grip succeed" sends the move, then polls until the state is 1, 2 or 3, with a timeout of its own; the ROS 2 driver and the PAROL6 software do exactly that.

    What the current limit does¶

    The current limit of a move is the driver's Iq limit for the duration of the move and the hold. It bounds the torque, so the force on an object, and it also bounds how fast the jaws accelerate: a low limit with a high speed gives a gentle, slow-starting move. The temperature of the motor is the other bound: holding an object at a high current for minutes heats the windings, and at 75 °C the thermistor stops the gripper.

    Timing¶

    The driver runs its loops at 6250 Hz; a move command takes effect at the next cycle, and the answer leaves within a millisecond. The CAN bus at 1 Mbit/s carries a command and its answer in well under half a millisecond, so a host can run the gripper at several hundred commands per second; 50 Hz is a good rate for a controller that polls the state.

    Over CAN¶

    CAN is the gripper's interface: the one cable carries it next to the power, and a robot's control board, a PC with the CANvas adapter or any microcontroller with a CAN transceiver drives the gripper with it. The gripper is a STEPFOC driver on the bus, so the frames are the driver's; this page is the part a gripper needs.

    The frame¶

    CAN 2.0 data frames with 11-bit identifiers at 1 Mbit/s. The identifier carries the node, the command and the error state:

    • Node ID, bits 10 to 7: which device. Every gripper ships as node 0; set another with Send_CAN_ID or #CANID and save it. Use 0 to 13: the driver's bootloader reserves 14 and 15, and a device on one of those cannot be updated over CAN.
    • Command ID, bits 6 to 1: which message. The gripper command is 61, its answer 60, the calibration 62.
    • Error bit, bit 0: in a frame from the gripper, 1 while any error is active. In a frame from the host it is ignored.

    So the gripper command to node 0 travels on identifier (0 << 7) | (61 << 1) = 0x7A, and the answer comes back on (60 << 1) | error = 0x78 or 0x79; to node 3 the command is 0x1FA and the answer 0x1F8. Multi-byte values are big-endian; flags inside a byte are numbered from the most significant bit.

    The messages a gripper needs¶

    Message ID What for
    Send_gripper_data_pack 61 the move command: position, speed, current, the activate, action and E-stop bits; 0 bytes to poll
    Respond_Gripper_data_pack 60 the answer: position, current, the state and error bits
    Send_gripper_calibrate 62 the gripper calibration, after every power-up
    Send_Clear_Error 1 clears the errors
    Send_ESTOP, Send_Idle 0, 12 stop
    Send_Watchdog 15 the gripper stops when the host stops talking
    Send_CAN_ID, Send_Save_config 11, 13 another node ID, saved
    Send_Respond_State_of_Errors, Send_Respond_Temperature 26, 23 the driver's error flags, the motor temperature

    The CAN reference has the bytes of each; the driver's other messages, which a gripper does not need, are on the STEPFOC site.

    A control loop¶

    1. At power-up: Send_Clear_Error, then Send_gripper_calibrate; poll with empty Send_gripper_data_pack frames until the calibrated bit is 1 (about ten seconds).
    2. For each grip: one Send_gripper_data_pack with the position, speed and current and the activate and action bits set; then poll with empty frames until the object-detection field reads 1, 2 or 3, or your own timeout passes.
    3. Keep polling at a steady rate (50 Hz is plenty) if you set the watchdog: every frame resets it, and the gripper stops by itself when your program stops.

    The answer to every frame tells the whole state, so a host never has to ask twice.

    From a PC¶

    The CANvas adapter shows up as a serial port and speaks slcan; python-can opens it with bustype='slcan', and the Python library wraps the messages. Any CAN adapter that python-can supports works the same way.

    On a bus with other devices¶

    A gripper on a robot shares the bus with the robot's joints or other grippers. Give each device its own node ID (connect one at a time, Send_CAN_ID, Send_Save_config), terminate both ends of the bus (CAN termination), and keep in mind that a lower node ID wins the bus arbitration.

    Over UART¶

    The driver inside the gripper has a serial port. It is not the gripper's everyday interface, which is CAN, but it is the way to configure the driver of a self-built gripper and the quickest way to look at what the driver reports when something is wrong.

    Reaching the port¶

    The UART connector of the driver (JST SH, 4 pins, 3.3 V) is inside the housing, under the coupler plate. Unscrew the plate, plug in the USB-to-serial adapter with the UART cable, and open a terminal at 256000 baud with CR+LF line endings; the gripper's 24 V must be on for anything but reading settings. The serial terminal on the STEPFOC site walks through the terminal setup.

    Danger

    The port is 3.3 V. A 5 V adapter destroys the driver.

    With Python¶

    The Spectral-BLDC library wraps the driver's CAN protocol, gripper messages included: one method per message, and the gripper's answers unpacked into attributes. The ROS 2 driver and the legacy gripper GUI are built on it.

    Installing¶

    pip install Spectral-BLDC

    The source and the examples are in Spectral-BLDC-Python.

    The first program¶

    import Spectral_BLDC as Spectral
import time

# the adapter: a CANvas on COM20 (Windows) or /dev/ttyACM0 (Linux), 1 Mbit/s
bus = Spectral.CanCommunication(bustype='slcan', channel='COM20', bitrate=1000000)
gripper = Spectral.SpectralCAN(node_id=0, communication=bus)

gripper.Send_Clear_Error()
gripper.Send_gripper_calibrate()
time.sleep(10)                                  # the jaws open fully, then close fully

for position in (200, 20, 200, 20):
    # position 0..255, speed 0..255, current mA, activate, action, E-stop, release direction
    gripper.Send_gripper_data_pack(position, 30, 300, 1, 1, 0, 0)
    for _ in range(40):                         # poll the state for up to 2 s
        gripper.Send_gripper_data_pack()        # an empty frame: status only
        message, ids = bus.receive_can_messages(timeout=0.05)
        if message is not None:
            gripper.UnpackData(message, ids)
            print(gripper.gripper_position, gripper.gripper_current, gripper.gripper_object_detection)
            if gripper.gripper_object_detection in (1, 2, 3):   # object found, or at the position
                break

    Send_gripper_data_pack with all seven arguments sends the move; without arguments it sends the empty status poll. receive_can_messages returns the raw python-can message and the node ID, command ID and error bit from the identifier; UnpackData fills the gripper attributes:

    Attribute Meaning
    gripper_position 0 open to 255 closed
    gripper_current the motor current in mA
    gripper_activated, gripper_action_status the activate flag; 1 while a move is in progress
    gripper_object_detection 0 moving, 1 object found while closing, 2 object found while opening, 3 at the position
    gripper_temperature_error, gripper_timeout_error, gripper_estop_error the three error bits
    gripper_calibrated the gripper calibration is done

    Object detection in library 1.20

    Library version 1.20 assembles gripper_object_detection with its two bits swapped relative to the firmware: it reports 1 where the gripper sends 2 (object found while opening) and 2 where it sends 1 (object found while closing); 0 and 3 are unaffected. Until the library is fixed, swap the two values in your program, or test for "1 or 2" as this example does.

    Stopping¶

    gripper.Send_gripper_data_pack(20, 30, 300, 1, 1, 1, 0)   # the E-stop bit: stops, raises the E-stop error
gripper.Send_Clear_Error()                                 # clears it; the next command must have the bit at 0
gripper.Send_Watchdog(500)                                 # from now on the gripper stops 500 ms after the last frame

    With the watchdog set, keep the status poll running at a steady rate; a program that crashes releases the grip within the watchdog time.

    Several grippers¶

    One CanCommunication per bus, one SpectralCAN per gripper with its node ID; every answer carries the node ID in ids.node_id, so route it to the right object. The teleoperation guide couples two grippers this way.

    The complete map of calls to messages is in the Python library reference.

    Errors and recovery¶

    When the driver inside the gripper detects a fault it stops the motor, goes to idle and sets the error bit in every CAN frame it sends. The grip is released: an idle stepper holds nothing beyond the mechanism's friction.

    Seeing the error¶

    • The error bit of every answer's identifier: 1 while any error is active.
    • Respond_Gripper_data_pack carries the three gripper error bits: temperature, timeout, E-stop.
    • Send_Respond_State_of_Errors returns every flag of the driver, which is where the other causes show.
    • Motor GUI shows the errors in its gripper panel; #Error over UART prints them.

    The errors¶

    Error Reported in Cause What to do
    Temperature the gripper's temperature bit the thermistor on the motor housing reads more than 75 °C (#Tlim) let the motor cool; hold with less current
    Timeout the gripper's timeout bit the driver's CAN watchdog ran out: no valid frame within the time set by Send_Watchdog the host stopped sending; fix the host, then clear
    E-stop the gripper's E-stop bit a move command with the E-stop bit set, or #Gripstop 1 clear, then send a command with the bit at 0: a command with the bit still set raises it again
    Not calibrated (motor) State_of_Errors, #Error the driver's own motor calibration is missing: a self-built gripper before Driver configuration, or after #Default calibrate the motor
    Vbus State_of_Errors the supply is below 9 V or above 29.5 V the supply, the cable
    Encoder State_of_Errors the driver's encoder does not see its magnet the magnet on the stepper shaft (a self-built gripper)
    Driver State_of_Errors the driver stage's own protection tripped: overcurrent, overtemperature lower the current; check the motor leads
    Velocity State_of_Errors the motor exceeded the driver's speed limit unlikely in a gripper; clear
    Driver E-stop State_of_Errors Send_ESTOP was received clear

    A gripper that is not gripper-calibrated is not in error: it just refuses moves until Send_gripper_calibrate has found the end stops.

    Clearing¶

    Send_Clear_Error, Clear in the GUI or #Clear clears every flag, the gripper's E-stop included, and goes to idle. An error whose cause is still present comes straight back: a hot motor, a supply that is still low. After an E-stop, the next move command must carry the E-stop bit at 0, or the error is raised again at once.

    After clearing, the gripper is idle and still calibrated and activated; the next move command moves it.

    When the gripper does not answer¶

    • No answer at all: the supply, the cable (and its pinout), the bus termination (about 60 Ω across CANH and CANL with everything unpowered), 1 Mbit/s on the adapter, the node ID.
    • Answers but does not move: not calibrated, not activated, an error active, or the action bit at 0.
    • Moves to one end during calibration and stops there: the other end stop is blocked, or the mechanism is too stiff for the calibration's 500 mA; after 24 seconds the routine gives up.

    Troubleshooting goes through the symptoms one by one.

    Maintenance¶

    The gripper needs little: every wearing part is printed, so a worn part is replaced by printing a new one, and the mechanism is a rail, two racks and a pinion that want a little grease now and then. Power the gripper off before any work on it.

    Before each session¶

    • The jaws sit tight on their carriers, nothing is cracked, the pads or the sponge tape are whole.
    • The cable's M8 nut is tight, the cable has slack for the wrist's full motion and runs clear of the jaws.
    • Nothing lies in the jaws' travel, and the gripper calibration finds both end stops.

    When the motion gets rough, and every few months of regular use¶

    • Grease. A little lithium grease on the rail and on the rack and pinion. Remove the four screws that hold the linear rail to reach the mechanism, wipe off the old grease and the dust, grease, refit, and move the jaws over the whole stroke by hand before you power up. A grinding noise, a stiff motion or a calibration that stops short of an end are the signs that it is due.
    • Clean with a dry cloth. The gripper is not waterproof or dustproof: keep it out of wet and dusty places, and blow chips out of the rail rather than wash them out.
    • Screws: the jaw screws, the rail screws and the clamp screws of the shaft coupler; threadlocker where the assembly instructions use it.
    • The camera lens, with a lens cloth.

    Replacing worn parts¶

    The jaws, the pads and every other printed part are in the product repository, Print files; the rail, the carriages, the pinion and the screws are in the BOM. A replacement jaw is the same part as a new one, and the gripper calibration at the next power-up takes care of the end stops.

    A stepper or a driver is not serviced but replaced: the stepper by one of the three in the BOM, after which the motor calibration of Driver configuration is run again; the driver by a new STEPFOC driver, configured the same way.

    Firmware¶

    The gripper has no firmware of its own: it runs the STEPFOC driver's firmware with gripper mode turned on. This site describes the firmware release named in the version box on the landing page, for the gripper release the site is versioned by; a new firmware release for the same gripper updates these pages in place.

    Which release does my gripper run?¶

    Over CAN, Send_Respond_Device_Info returns the firmware version as its third byte; over UART, #Info prints Software version: <number>. The compatibility table says which releases go with which Python library and bootloader.

    Updating¶

    A gripper shipped with firmware 104 or newer carries the driver's bootloader and updates over its CAN cable: the host tells the driver to reset, the bootloader waits two seconds for a host, and the host streams the new image into it. Nothing is opened. The image is stepfoc-firmware-V<number>.bin from the driver's GitHub releases, with a manifest firmware.json and the release notes.

    From How
    a PC with the CANvas adapter Motor GUI over CAN: choose the release, the GUI fetches the image and flashes it
    a PC, scripted host_tools/bl_flash.py from the bootloader repository, over CAN (slcan or gs_usb adapters) or over the driver's UART port
    a PAR6 control box the box updates every device on its bus, the gripper included, from its dashboard or tools/flash_motor_firmware.py

    The procedure, the bootloader's protocol and what to do when a flash is interrupted are in the STEPFOC documentation: Updating through the bootloader and The bootloader.

    Before you update

    • Open the gripper or take the object out: it loses its grip during the update and until it is calibrated and commanded again.
    • An update may clear the driver's configuration (the release notes say so when it does). A bought gripper's configuration is in Driver configuration, so it can be restored; note what #Info prints before you start.
    • Give the gripper a node ID from 0 to 13: the bootloader cannot address 14 or 15.

    A gripper whose driver runs firmware 103 or older has no bootloader; it is updated once with a programming adapter on the driver's JTAG pads, inside the housing, as described in Installing the bootloader on an older board. From then on it updates over CAN like any other.

    Troubleshooting¶

    By symptom. The error flags and what clears them are in Errors and recovery.

    The gripper does not answer¶

    Symptom Check
    nothing at all the 24 V on the cable; the cable's pinout (a wrong one has destroyed the driver already); the node ID the host uses; 1 Mbit/s on the adapter; the termination (about 60 Ω across CANH and CANL, unpowered)
    answers, then stops answering the watchdog ran out and the host did not notice; a loose M8 connector; a second device with the same node ID
    the GUI shows No connection the COM port of the CANvas adapter; another program holding the port; the CAN ID in the GUI

    It does not move¶

    flowchart TD

    B -- no --> C[24 V on the cable, the pinout, the node ID,<br>1 Mbit/s, the termination]

    D -- no --> E[Run the gripper calibration;<br>a self-built gripper: the stepper calibration first]

    calibrated, activated, no error, no motion the action bit of the command is 0; the command is identical to the previous one (change the speed or the current by one to repeat a move)

    an error is shown Errors and recovery: clear it, then look at the cause if it comes back
    the driver reports Not calibrated in State_of_Errors and #Error the stepper itself is not calibrated (a self-built gripper): Driver configuration

    Calibration¶

    Symptom Cause
    the jaws move to one end and stop; after 24 s the gripper is still not calibrated the other end is blocked, or the mechanism is too stiff for the calibration's 500 mA: a tight rail, a pinion pressed too hard into the racks, debris in the racks
    the jaws jitter and do not travel a phase of the stepper not connected (self-built), or the stepper not calibrated
    255 is not fully closed, or 0 not fully open something stopped the jaws short of the end during calibration; calibrate again with the stroke free

    The grip¶

    Symptom Cause
    the object slips the current too low for the object; the jaw faces: sponge tape or the TPU pad
    the object is crushed the current too high; a hard object takes the full force at once
    object detected while nothing is there the current limit too low for the mechanism's friction at that speed; raise the current a little or lower the speed
    the gripper stops with a temperature error after a while a long hold at a high current; hold with the lowest current that keeps the object
    the jaws creep or hum when holding the position loop against a hard object; lower the current, or hold a soft object with a position slightly beyond the contact

    Noise and heat¶

    A stepper under field-oriented control is quiet; a grinding noise is the rack and pinion without grease, or debris. The motor gets warm in use and hot under a long grip; the thermistor stops it at 75 °C. A self-built gripper without the thermistor on its motor has no such protection.

    After a firmware update¶

    If the gripper does not calibrate or behaves oddly after an update, check the driver's configuration with #Info over UART: gripper mode (#Gripper 1), the thermistor (#Term 1), the motor calibration. A release note says when an update clears the configuration; Driver configuration restores it.

    Driver setup and first run¶

    A gripper from the shop arrives with its driver configured: the stepper calibrated, gripper mode on, the thermistor on, the gains set, everything saved. A gripper you build has a bare STEPFOC driver that must be brought to the same state once, over its UART port. This page is that procedure; the commands are the driver's, and the STEPFOC documentation explains each in depth.

    What you need¶

    • The driver mounted on the stepper with its encoder magnet, before the pinion is engaged in the racks: the motor calibration turns the motor several revolutions in both directions, which the racks cannot follow. Do this step with the stepper out of the gripper or with the pinion not yet fitted.
    • A 3.3 V USB-to-serial adapter on the driver's UART connector, a terminal at 256000 baud with CR+LF line endings, and 24 V on the driver's power connector. The serial terminal on the STEPFOC site shows the terminal setup.
    • The thermistor from the BOM, soldered to the driver's thermistor connector and fixed to the motor housing with thermal cement.

    With Motor GUI¶

    Motor GUI, connected over UART through the serial adapter, has a preset per stepper on its setup page: MSG 21.5 mm motor, MSG 40 mm motor and MSG 60 mm motor. A preset writes the stepper's resistance, inductance and 50 pole pairs, the current limit, the loop gains, gripper mode, the thermistor and the CAN ID, then saves; its CAN ID is 6, the PAR6 gripper's, so change it before applying for another robot. Apply it after the motor calibration of step 1: it does step 2 for you.

    Current limit¶

    The gripper's moves use the current limit of each command, not the driver's #Ilim. Keep the commanded current at or below the stepper's maximum current (Force and speed); the firmware does not clamp it.

    STEPFOC firmware V106 — application image for flashing via the Spectral bootloader.

    CAN bus¶

    • The controller now drains up to 8 CAN messages per pass instead of just 1, so short bursts (multiple commands sent back-to-back, or a busy multi-node bus) no longer silently drop messages once the 3-deep hardware FIFO fills.
    • A CAN peripheral that fails to initialize no longer bricks the board — UART stays fully usable, only the CAN bus is skipped.

    Safety¶

    • Added a hardware watchdog (100ms). If the control loop ever stops running, the board resets instead of leaving the motor driven at a stale duty cycle.
    • Fixed a bug where Torque_mode could silently overwrite your commanded Iq setpoint when clamping to the current limit — reading it back after clamping now shows what you actually asked for, and raising the current limit afterward immediately restores the original commanded torque instead of requiring the command to be resent.
    • Fixed undefined behavior in a serial-protocol integer conversion (fourBytesToInt).
    • Synced default configuration values that had drifted between two locations in the code, so a fresh #Default and a freshly-flashed board's built-in defaults now agree.

    EEPROM / configuration integrity¶

    • Added CRC validation over the saved configuration, split into two independent halves: calibration data (resistance, inductance, Kt/KV, angle offset, current-loop gains, etc.) and settings (PID tuning, limits, watchdog config, CAN ID, etc.).
    • This means a corrupted save (e.g. from a power loss mid-write) is detected and safely defaulted instead of silently running with garbage values — and critically, a future firmware update that only adds a new setting can no longer wipe your calibration, since the two are validated independently.
    • Boards upgrading from older, pre-CRC firmware keep their existing calibration automatically — no need to recalibrate after this update.
    • A few unused slots were reserved in the calibration block for upcoming features (e.g. magnet alignment), so those can be added later without another migration.

    Performance¶

    Glossary¶

    Action bit
    A bit of the move command: 1 carries out the move, 0 leaves the gripper idle with the command stored.
    Activated
    A flag the gripper keeps, set by the activate bit of the move command or by #Gripact. A move is carried out only while the gripper is activated and calibrated.
    CAN ID (node ID)
    The address of the gripper on the CAN bus, 0 to 13; every gripper ships as 0. It occupies bits 10 to 7 of the 11-bit CAN frame identifier; the command ID occupies bits 6 to 1 and the error bit is bit 0.
    CANvas
    Source Robotics' USB-to-CAN adapter. It appears as a serial port and speaks slcan, which python-can, Motor GUI and the ROS 2 driver use.
    Current limit (grip force)
    The current, in mA, the driver may put into the motor during a move. Torque is current times the motor's torque constant, so the current limit sets the force with which the jaws press on an object.
    Driver
    The STEPFOC board on the back of the gripper's stepper: field-oriented control of the motor, the gripper state machine and the CAN and UART interfaces. The gripper's firmware is the driver's firmware.
    E-stop bit
    A bit of the move command. 1 stops the gripper and raises the gripper E-stop error, which stays until it is cleared and a command with the bit at 0 arrives.
    Error bit
    Bit 0 of every CAN frame identifier the driver sends: 1 while any error is active.
    Field-oriented control (FOC)
    Controlling the stepper's current as a vector relative to the rotor, from the driver's encoder. It turns the stepper into a motor whose torque is known and limited, which is what makes a force-controlled gripper out of it.
    Firmware release
    One build of the STEPFOC firmware, numbered 103, 104, 105, … and tagged V103, V104, … in the driver's repository. #Info prints the release a gripper runs. This documentation describes one release; the version selector switches between them.
    Gripper calibration
    The routine (Send_gripper_calibrate, #Gripcal) that opens the jaws fully, then closes them fully, at low force, and records both end stops as positions 0 and 255. Needed after every power-up.
    Gripper mode
    The driver's mode in which it runs a gripper (#Gripper 1, saved): move commands with a jaw position, a speed and a current limit, and object detection.
    Motor calibration
    The driver's own calibration of the stepper (#Cal): the pole pairs, resistance and inductance entered, the encoder aligned, the motor constants measured. Done once, at the factory or when you build the gripper; not to be confused with the gripper calibration.
    Object detection
    The gripper's report of why the jaws stopped: 0 still moving, 1 an object stopped them while closing, 2 an object stopped them while opening, 3 they reached the commanded position.
    Position
    The jaw position as the gripper commands and reports it: 0 fully open, 255 fully closed, linear in between.
    Rack and pinion
    The gripper's mechanism: a pinion on the motor shaft meshes with two racks that run on the linear rail in opposite directions, so one motor moves both jaws symmetrically.
    Speed
    The speed of a move, 0 to 255, mapped by the firmware to 500 to 60000 encoder ticks per second of the motor.
    Stroke
    How far each jaw carrier travels from fully open to fully closed: 55, 105 or 155 mm, set by the rail length.
    Timeout error (watchdog)
    The driver's CAN watchdog, as the gripper reports it: when the watchdog is set (Send_Watchdog) and no valid frame arrives in time, the gripper stops and raises it.
    Watchdog
    A timer the driver resets with every valid CAN frame it receives. When it is set and runs out, the gripper reports a timeout error and stops.

    API reference

    API reference¶

    The gripper's CAN messages and UART commands, its status and error bits, and the Python library calls that send them, for the firmware named on the landing page. The gripper is a STEPFOC stepper driver in gripper mode and accepts the driver's whole command set; only what a gripper host or a self-built gripper needs is here, the rest is in the STEPFOC reference.

    • CAN messages: the identifier, and the gripper, system and status messages
    • UART commands: the gripper commands, the driver setup commands, status and utility
    • Errors and status bits
    • Python library: which library call sends which message

    Conventions. Bytes are numbered from 0 and multi-byte values are big-endian. Bits are numbered from the most significant: bit 7 is the first flag in a byte. The position is 0 (open) to 255 (closed), the speed 0 to 255, the current in mA. UART command names are case-sensitive.

    The command IDs, frame lengths and responses are read from the firmware's source at its release tag when the site is built, so they cannot drift from the firmware; the descriptions and byte layouts are written by hand.

    CAN frame tool and downloads¶

    Build or inspect a frame using the command definitions of this manual's firmware release. All calculations run in your browser; the tool does not connect to an adapter or send commands to hardware.

    Downloads¶

    Command definitions (JSON) · Gripper DBC

    The JSON includes the firmware tag, source commit, message IDs, every documented layout and its bit order. The DBC files cover nodes 0 to 13, with both the clean and fault-bit identifiers of driver replies. Reserved commands are omitted. Values use the firmware's native units: ticks, ticks/s, mA and the units in the CAN reference.

    An RTR request uses the same identifier as its data reply. DBC describes the data reply; it does not represent the request's RTR flag or the empty gripper status poll. Both are supported by the browser tool.

    The gripper's two object-detection bits have an unusual order: bit 5 is the low bit, bit 4 the high bit. The DBC signal gripper_object_detection_raw retains the wire value and supplies text labels (raw 2 = object while closing, raw 1 = object while opening). The browser decoder converts that pair to the firmware's numeric status (1 = closing, 2 = opening).

    CAN messages¶
    

    CAN 2.0, 1 Mbit/s, 11-bit identifiers. The identifier carries the node ID (bits 10 to 7, 0 to 13), the command ID

explains how the pieces are used; this page lists them.

  the others are not answered.

  the gripper answers with a data frame under the same command ID.
      • 

    • Respond_… — frames only the gripper sends.
      • 

    

    Multi-byte values are big-endian (data[0] first); bits inside a byte are numbered from the most significant bit
(bit 7 first).

    Basic control in Python¶
    

    The shortest program that drives the gripper: it connects through the CANvas adapter, clears the errors,
calibrates, then closes and opens every three seconds and prints what the gripper answers. It is the example from
the product repository's README; pip install Spectral-BLDC is the only requirement. Change COM20 to your
adapter's port (/dev/ttyACM0 on Linux) and node_id to the gripper's CAN ID if you changed it from 0.
    

    

    Danger
    

    700 mA closes the jaws with real force, about 14 N with the 21.5 mm stepper and 25 N with the 60 mm. Keep fingers out of the jaws, and start with a lower current
if the gripper is new to you.
    

    

    import Spectral_BLDC as Spectral
import time

Communication1 = Spectral.CanCommunication(bustype='slcan', channel='COM20', bitrate=1000000)
Motor1 = Spectral.SpectralCAN(node_id=0, communication=Communication1)

Motor1.Send_Clear_Error()
Motor1.Send_gripper_calibrate()
time.sleep(4)

temp_var = 0
while True:
   if temp_var == 0:
      Motor1.Send_gripper_data_pack(50,100,700,1,1,0,0)
      temp_var = 1
   elif temp_var == 1:
      Motor1.Send_gripper_data_pack(240,100,700,1,1,0,0)
      temp_var = 0

   message, UnpackedMessageID = Communication1.receive_can_messages(timeout=0.2)

   if message is not None:
      print(f"Message is: {message}")
      print(f"Node ID is : {UnpackedMessageID.node_id}")
      print(f"Message ID is: {UnpackedMessageID.command_id}")
      print(f"Error bit is: {UnpackedMessageID.error_bit}")
      print(f"Timestamp is: {message.timestamp}")

   time.sleep(3)

    

    The arguments of Send_gripper_data_pack, in order: the position (0 open, 255 closed), the speed (0 to 255), the
current limit in mA (the force), then the activate bit, the action bit, the E-stop bit and the release direction.
With Python explains the library's calls, How the gripper works
the state the gripper answers with, and Force and speed what the
current means in newtons.
    Teleoperation with two grippers¶
    

    Two grippers on one CAN bus, coupled so that each follows the other: squeeze one by hand and the other closes;
when the other meets an object, the one in your hand pushes back with the force it feels. A hundred lines of
Python, and the basis of a teleoperation setup with force feedback.
    

    This guide does not use the gripper mode. Both drivers run in current (torque) mode, the driver's own motion
command, and the PC closes a position loop between them at a fixed rate. The gripper mode's position, speed and
object detection are not involved; the drivers are plain torque sources here.
    

    What you need¶
    

      

    • Two grippers, calibrated as motors (a bought gripper is), with different node IDs: 0 and 1 (#CANID 1,
  #Save on one of them over UART).
      • 

    • Both on one bus with the CANvas adapter, 24 V on both, termination at the two ends.
      • 

    • pip install Spectral-BLDC SourceRoboticsToolbox.
      • 

    

    

    Warning
    

    In current mode the gripper has no end-stop knowledge and no object detection: it pushes with whatever current
the loop commands. Set the driver's current limit to the stepper's rating (#Ilim) on both grippers, start
with low gains, and keep fingers out of the jaws.
    

    

    The idea¶
    

    Each driver is commanded a current proportional to the difference between the two positions, plus a damping
term on the speed difference and a little damping of its own motion:
    

    \[
I_0 = K_p\,(p_1 - p_0) + K_d\,(v_1 - v_0) - K_k\, v_0, \qquad
I_1 = K_p\,(p_0 - p_1) + K_d\,(v_0 - v_1) - K_k\, v_1
\]
    

    \(p\) is the jaw position of each gripper in the same units, \(v\) its speed. When the two positions agree, both
currents are zero and the grippers rest. Move one by hand and the difference drives the other after it; block
the other, and the difference drives the one in your hand back with the same current, which is the force you
feel. \(K_p\) is the stiffness of the virtual spring between them, \(K_d\) its damping.
    

    The two drivers' encoders do not read the same number at the same jaw position, so each position is referred to
a reference reading taken once with both grippers in the same position; the Source Robotics Toolbox's Joint
does that bookkeeping and unwraps the encoder over its 16384-tick turns.
    

    The code¶
    

    import time
import numpy as np
import Spectral_BLDC as Spectral
import SourceRoboticsToolbox as Toolbox

INTERVAL_S = 0.002                 # the loop rate: 500 Hz
REFERENCE = [10440, 13555]         # each gripper's raw encoder reading with both jaws in the same position

Kp, Kd, Kk = 650, 0.0055, 0.0003   # stiffness, damping between the two, damping of each

bus = Spectral.CanCommunication(bustype='slcan', channel='COM123', bitrate=1000000)
motors = [Spectral.SpectralCAN(node_id=i, communication=bus) for i in (0, 1)]
joints = [Toolbox.Joint(encoder_resolution=14, master_position=REFERENCE[i], gear_ratio=1, offset=0, dir=0)
          for i in (0, 1)]

position = np.zeros(2)             # each gripper's position, referred to its reference
sector_known = [False, False]
current = [0, 0]

while True:
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    for i in (0, 1):               # command the currents; each answer carries position and speed
        motors[i].Send_data_pack_1(Position=None, Speed=None, Current=int(current[i]))
    for _ in (0, 1):
        message, ids = bus.receive_can_messages(timeout=0.001)
        if ids is None:
            continue
        i = ids[0]
        motors[i].UnpackData(message, ids)
        if not sector_known[i]:
            joints[i].determine_sector(motors[i].position)
            sector_known[i] = True
        position[i] = joints[i].get_joint_position(motors[i].position)

    current[0] = Kp * (position[1] - position[0]) + Kd * (motors[1].speed - motors[0].speed) - Kk * motors[0].speed
    current[1] = Kp * (position[0] - position[1]) + Kd * (motors[0].speed - motors[1].speed) - Kk * motors[1].speed

    time.sleep(max(0.0, INTERVAL_S - (time.perf_counter() - t0)))

    

    The reference readings: with both grippers powered and their jaws set by hand to the same opening, read each
driver's position (#Info over UART, or Send_Respond_Encoder_data from Python) and put the two numbers in
REFERENCE.
    

    Tuning¶
    

    Start with Kp at a tenth of the value above and raise it until the follower tracks the leader without lagging;
raise Kd if the pair oscillates. The current the formula produces is in mA and is clamped by each driver's
#Ilim, so the stepper's rating is the ceiling of the force feedback. A fixed loop rate matters: a loop that
runs at an uneven rate makes the damping terms noisy.
    

    The full example, with a timer for a precise loop rate, is
gripper_teleop_PC_calc.py
in the Python library's examples, and the bilateral teleoperation guide
on the STEPFOC site explains the control law with a joint instead of a gripper.

    Report a problem¶
    

    Open an issue in the product repository
for the gripper and its files, in the driver's repository
for the firmware, or in Spectral-BLDC-Python
for the Python library. Include:
    

      

    1. The gripper: bought or self-built, the rail and the stepper, the jaws.
      2. 

    2. The firmware release the gripper runs: Send_Respond_Device_Info, or #Info over UART, which also carries
   the driver's configuration and errors.
      3. 

    3. The gripper's status when the problem happens: the state and error bits of Respond_Gripper_data_pack, or
   #Gripinfo.
      4. 

    4. What you did and what happened: the commands, in order, with their position, speed and current, and the
   answers.
      5. 

    5. How it is connected: the robot or the adapter, the cable, the termination; a photo helps more than a
   description.
      6. 

    

    For a problem with this documentation, the Report it link at the end of the page opens an issue in the
documentation repository with the page already named.

    How the site is built¶
    

      

    • The pages are Markdown, built with MkDocs and the Material theme; the repository's README.md says how to
  preview it locally.
      • 

    • The site describes one release of the STEPFOC firmware, named in versions.yml. The command IDs, frame
  lengths, the UART command list and what each prints in the API reference are read from the firmware's source at
  that release's tag by tools/sync_firmware.py; do not edit those facts in the pages. The descriptions of the
  commands are in docs/includes/can_commands.yml and uart_commands.yml, which list only the commands a
  gripper needs.

  site under its firmware version with mike.

    House rules¶
    

      

    • Facts come from the firmware and the product repository. When a page and the firmware disagree, the firmware
  is right; fix the page, or report the firmware.

    • Spell commands exactly as the firmware does, even where the spelling looks wrong.
